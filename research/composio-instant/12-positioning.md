## The patterns

Four recurring moves, in rising order of how much work they do:

1. **Category label** — "Ultimate AI Media Generation Platform". Says what shelf. Does nothing.
2. **Noun-for-noun** — "The world's data for AI agents", "Generative media for agents". Clear, instantly legible, and by now a template. Everyone can fill it in.
3. **Imperative with a verb the buyer already wants** — "Run AI with an API", "Give your agents access to the whole web". Puts the reader in motion.
4. **A thesis** — "The future is multi-model". Takes a position the reader can agree or disagree with, which is why it's memorable.

## The problem with "Generative media for agents"

It is literally fal's noun ("generative media") in Browserbase's grammar ("for agents"). Both halves are borrowed, and neither is a claim — it's a shelf label. It is accurate, which is why it feels safe, and inert, which is why it doesn't land. It also leaves the actual product on the floor: the interesting thing about Sequel is not that media exists for agents, it's that the agent *picks* the model instead of the developer hardcoding one, and that every run comes back with its cost attached.

Secondary risk: Krea is already saying "the intelligent Krea Agent. The future of generative media." The generic agent framing is contested ground; the specific one is not.

## What's actually ours to claim

- The agent describes the job, not the model. Semantic search over the catalogue is the product.
- Schemas on demand — it never guesses a parameter name.
- One connection instead of a drawer of API keys.
- Every run priced, attributed and auditable. One bill.
- MCP-native: nothing to wire, no SDK to learn.

The two no one else is saying out loud: **the agent chooses** and **every run is accounted for**.
